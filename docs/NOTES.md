## Simplifications in the films and games
- Orbits are drawn as flat rings (Bohr's model, as in the book). The orbital clouds in film 05 show the shapes only;
  the f-orbital is drawn as a "complicated shape" (8 lobes), as the book says, not as a real f-orbital.
- Film 05 "Clouds" act (30–82 s) and game Electron Trails use only the book's words (p.49): the electron revolves round
  the nucleus in a fixed shell; a sub-shell is the area in which it moves within its shell. The laps that tilt and
  change size (filling a ball, or loops inside the p/d lobes that switch lobes by passing beside the nucleus) are a
  teaching picture of "moves within an area"; real electrons have no path that can be followed (beyond the book).
  2s and 2p are both shell-L size; the areas share space, the limit is 2 electrons per orbital (book's capacity rule).
- The marble cases in film 09 round the percentages to whole marbles: Cl 75.53/24.47 → 76/24 and U 99.245/0.75 →
  99/1. U-234 (0.05 %) and C-14 (1 part per trillion) get less than one marble.
- Film 08's "20,000 dots, 3 gold" picture is 0.015 % exactly.
- Energy order 4s < 3d is the book's Aufbau rule for filling. It holds for K and Ca; real atoms can be more complex.
- The Book cap row in the games (2, 8, 8, 32 …) follows the book's shell filling for the first 20 elements.
- Prism bench (Light Jumps level 1): the glass spreads colours about 4× more than real glass (n = 1.40 + 30000/λ²) so
  the rainbow fits on a small table. The bending itself (Snell's law, total internal reflection) is real.

## Unit 8 (book errors and gaps)
- Sodium's density: Table 8.4 says 0.971 g/cm³, Table 8.7 says 0.927 g/cm³ (real: 0.968). Games use neither.
  Lesson 807 shows 0.971 (Table 8.4), lesson 813 shows 0.927 (Table 8.7), each as printed on its page.
- p.234 prints 2MgO + H₂O → 2Mg(OH)₂ and 2CaO + H₂O → 2Ca(OH)₂ (not balanced); games use CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂.
- Short Q viii asks for Cl₂ + NaOH, which the chapter text never gives. Game answer (cold, dilute):
  Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaOCl + H₂O.
- Table 8.3 lists Be–Ba only (no radium); film 806 and the games follow it.
- p.219 Scientific Tidbit is a simplification (old A-group numbers: "group 3" = IIIA …). Lesson 801 colours the table
  as printed: groups 4/5 only Sn, Pb / Sb, Bi blue (C, Si, Ge, N, P, As green), groups 6–8 all green (real: Po is a
  metalloid/metal; Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te are metalloids, film 802). Superheavy elements follow their column's rule.
  The book contradicts itself: p.219 calls antimony a metal, p.221 a metalloid (802 follows p.221).
- Lesson 801 shows sodium alight in air (book: "catches fire"); really it needs heating first, it only tarnishes cold.
- p.229 says conductivity decreases from IA to IIA; really magnesium conducts slightly better than sodium (about
  2.3 vs 2.1 × 10⁷ S/m). Lesson 806 shows the book's statement (exam answer).
- p.234 prints 2MgO + H₂O → 2Mg(OH)₂ and 2CaO + H₂O → 2Ca(OH)₂ (not balanced: 2 O + 1 O on the left, 4 O on the
  right). Correct: MgO + H₂O → Mg(OH)₂, CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂. Lesson 811 shows the balanced form (its atom balls must
  count); teachers may mention the book's version.
- p.239 Society box lists the platinum group as rhenium, rhodium, palladium, osmium, iridium, platinum. The platinum
  group is ruthenium (Ru), not rhenium (Re); p.236 itself lists ruthenium among the noble metals. Lesson 814 shows the
  box as printed (exam answer).
- p.239 Scientific Information: mercury's melting point is printed 38.87 °C (real −38.83 °C) and bromine's 7.2 °C
  (real −7.2 °C); the book also counts 27 non-metals (the groups it lists give 25 with period 7, 22 without).
  Lesson 815 shows the values and the count as printed (exam answers).
- p.242 Table 8.9 prints the densities in g/dm³, but the numbers (F 0.0017, Cl 0.0032, Br 3.1028, I 4.933) are g/cm³
  values (fluorine gas is 1.7 g/dm³ = 0.0017 g/cm³). Lesson 816 shows them as printed.
- p.245 the reactivity order is printed "F > Cl > Br > I > As": As is arsenic; the book means astatine (At). Lesson 817
  shows At. p.243 prints bromine's oxide as Br₃O₄ (the known oxide is Br₃O₈); lesson 817 shows it as printed.
